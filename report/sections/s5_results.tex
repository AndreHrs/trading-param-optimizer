The Kruskal--Wallis test was significant across all ten strategy $\times$
initialisation combinations on training fitness ($p < 10^{-34}$ in every
case; Table~\ref{tab:kruskal_wallis}). Three patterns emerge from Dunn's
pairwise post-hoc comparisons (Figure~\ref{fig:dunn_train}). First, GPS is
significantly separated from all population-based methods in the large
majority of conditions. The clearest exception is \texttt{ema\_independent},
where GPS and MRFO are not significantly different ($p = 0.16$ fixed,
$p = 1.00$ random); MRFO collapses to the no-trade fixed point in every
fixed-initialisation run ($\textrm{CV} = 0$), so the non-significance
reflects two algorithms failing to the same floor.

Second, the PSO--SOS--AOS cluster is strategy-dependent: largely
indistinguishable on \texttt{weighted} and $d=2$ strategies, but breaking
apart on \texttt{ema\_independent}, where AOS separates significantly from
PSO ($p = 1.7 \times 10^{-3}$ fixed, $p = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ random) and
from SOS.

Third, SOS exhibits zero within-cell variance on $d=2$ strategies
($\textrm{CV} = 0.000$; Table~\ref{tab:perf_train}), consistent with its
three-phase update collapsing diversity rapidly. Its mean wall-clock runtime
($14{,}730$\,ms) is roughly four times that of PSO ($3{,}706$\,ms).

\subsection{Test Results --- Statistical Differentiation}

%TC:ignore
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/dunn_pairwise_test.png}
  \caption{\label{fig:dunn_test}%
    Dunn's post-hoc pairwise $p$-values (Bonferroni-corrected) for all strategy
    $\times$ initialisation combinations --- test fitness.}
\end{figure}
%TC:endignore

On test data, the strong training differences largely collapse
(Figure~\ref{fig:dunn_test}; Table~\ref{tab:kruskal_wallis}). The most
extreme case is \texttt{ema\_shared}: every pairwise $p$-value equals $1.000$
and Kruskal--Wallis is non-significant under both initialisation modes ---
all six algorithms are statistically indistinguishable on the strategy that
produced the most baseline-beating runs.

The one strategy retaining strong test-side differentiation is
\texttt{ema\_independent} ($H = 184.26$ fixed, $179.13$ random). PSO and SOS
retain high test medians (pooled medians $\$4{,}435$ and $\$4{,}566$), whereas
AOS collapses to $\$1{,}105$ despite leading on training fitness
(Table~\ref{tab:perf_test}). A caution applies: on strategies where roughly
$32\%$ of runs sit at broken fixed points, statistical significance can
reflect how often an algorithm collapses rather than genuine optimisation
quality.

\subsection{Train--Test Rank Transfer}

%TC:ignore
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/train_test_spearman.png}
  \caption{\label{fig:spearman}%
    Spearman rank correlation between training and test fitness per strategy
    (both initialisation modes pooled, $n=600$ per strategy).
    $\rho = 0$ corresponds to no rank transfer.}
\end{figure}
%TC:endignore

Spearman rank correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:spearman}) reveals that
\texttt{sma} has the strongest train--test transfer ($\rho = 0.66$), while
\texttt{ema\_shared} shows none ($\rho = 0.05$, $p = 0.20$, n.s.). The
relationship is not monotonic in dimensionality: \texttt{ema\_shared}
($d=3$) transfers worse than the $d=14$ \texttt{weighted} space
($\rho = 0.34$), and decoupling the two EMAs' smoothing rates moves $\rho$
from $0.05$ to $0.38$ with no other change. The nature of the
parameterisation matters more than its size.

Notably, win rate is anti-correlated with transfer reliability: the two
strategies with the most baseline-beating runs (\texttt{ema\_shared},
\texttt{weighted}) have the weakest transfer, while \texttt{sma} --- the
strongest transfer --- produced zero wins. Table~\ref{tab:algo_run_details}
makes this concrete: the single highest test result ($\$9{,}615$, GPS
\texttt{ema\_shared}) came from a training fitness of only $\$16{,}290$ ---
an order of magnitude below runs that tested far lower.
